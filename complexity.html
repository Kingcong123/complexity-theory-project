Complexity Classes and the Strong Church–Turing Thesis

Since we now understand how to rigorously model quantum circuits, we can finally reason about the complexity of these circuits. Using this, we will introduce some basic complexity classes related to quantum circuits, and discuss the Strong Church–Turing Thesis.

Quantum Computational Complexity

Similar to how we can define the size of a Boolean circuit, we can define the size of a quantum circuit as the total number of gates it contains. We can also define the depth of a quantum circuit as the maximum number of gates along any directed path through the circuit.

Like Boolean circuits, quantum circuits are a non-uniform model of computation. A single quantum circuit operates on a fixed number of qubits, so we need to consider a family of circuits to perform computations on inputs of arbitrary length.

A quantum circuit family is a sequence $\{C_n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$, where $C_n$ is a quantum circuit that takes as input classical bit strings of length $n$. The family is said to be polynomial-size if there exists a polynomial $p(n)$ such that the size of $C_n$ is bounded by $p(n)$ for every $n$.

However, just as with Boolean circuits, non-uniformity gives us too much freedom in our computation. Therefore, we also require that the circuits must be constructed efficiently.

We say that a quantum circuit family is polynomial-time uniform if there exists a deterministic Turing machine $M$ such that, given $1^n$ as input, outputs a description of $C_n$ in polynomial time.

Now, what does it mean for a quantum circuit family to compute a function $f:\{0,1\}^*\to\{0,1\}$? The answer to this question is slightly more complicated than for Boolean circuits. Unlike a Boolean circuit, which has a deterministic output, the measurement performed on a quantum circuit's output is probabilistic.

First, we need to define how a quantum circuit outputs a classical bit. Given a classical input $x\in\{0,1\}^n$, we initialize the first $n$ qubits to $|x\rangle$. We may also use additional ancilla qubits initialized to $|0\rangle$. Therefore, if the circuit uses $m$ ancilla qubits, its initial state is $$ |\psi_0\rangle = |x\rangle\otimes|0\rangle^{\otimes m}. $$ After applying the circuit's unitary transformations, we measure one designated output qubit in the computational basis. The measurement outcome, either $0$ or $1$, is defined as the classical output of the circuit.

With all of this in place, we can now formally define what it means for a quantum family to compute a Boolean function.

Definition: Computing with Bounded Error. A quantum circuit family $\{C_n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ computes a function $f:\{0,1\}^*\to\{0,1\}$ with error $\varepsilon$ if, for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and every $x\in\{0,1\}^n$, $$ \Pr[C_n(x)=f(x)]\geq 1-\varepsilon. $$ Here, the probability is taken over the possible outcomes of the circuit's final measurement.

When $\varepsilon=0$, the circuit family computes $f$ exactly. For bounded-error computation, we typically require $\varepsilon < \frac{1}{2}$.

Bounded-Error Quantum Polynomial Time (BQP)

We can now introduce one of the most important complexity classes in quantum computation: $\mathsf{BQP}$, or Bounded-Error Quantum Polynomial Time. This is the class of languages that can be decided efficiently by a uniform quantum circuit family, with a bounded probability of error.

Definition: BQP. A language $L\subseteq\{0,1\}^*$ belongs to $\mathsf{BQP}$ if there exists a polynomial-time uniform family of quantum circuits $\{C_n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ such that, for every $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and $x\in\{0,1\}^n$, $$ \begin{aligned} x\in L &\implies \Pr[C_n(x)=1]\geq\frac{2}{3},\\ x\notin L &\implies \Pr[C_n(x)=0]\geq\frac{2}{3}. \end{aligned} $$ Here, $C_n(x)$ denotes the result of measuring a designated output qubit after applying $C_n$ to the initial state $|x\rangle\otimes|0\rangle^{\otimes m(n)}$, where $m(n)$ is some polynomial in $n$.

Similar to when we define complexity class $\mathsf{BPP}$, the The choice of $\frac{1}{3}$ as the error bound is arbitrary. Any fixed error bound in $(0, \frac{1}{2})$ defines the same complexity class. This again follows from the fact we can increase the probability of success by boosting.

One famous example of the power of quantum computation is integer factorization. Given a positive integer $N$, the goal is to find its prime factorization.

Shor's algorithm, discovered by Peter Shor in 1994, solves this problem on a quantum computer in polynomial time with respect to the length og $N$. Although integer factorization is technically a search problem rather than a language, Shor's algorithm implies that its associated decision problems are in $\mathsf{BQP}$.

This is a striking contrast with classical computation. The fastest known classical factoring methods, such as the General Number Field Sieve, have subexponential running times. No classical polynomial-time factoring algorithm is currently known, even if we allow randomized algorithms with bounded error (otherwise we would have some problems in cryptographic security).

While this does not prove that quantum computation is strictly more powerful than classical computation, it provides some evidence that quantum computation may offer some advantages. We will not explore Shor's algorithm in this project, but will instead study another important quantum algorithm: Grover's algorithm.

Relationships with Classical Complexity Classes

How does $\mathsf{BQP}$ relate to other classical complexity classes, such as $\mathsf{P}$, $\mathsf{NP}$, $\mathsf{BPP}$, $\mathsf{PSPACE}$, and $\mathsf{EXP}$?

As you may have guessed, any problem that can be solved efficiently by probabilistic classical computations can also be solved efficiently by quantum computations. This gives us our first theorem.

Theorem. $$ \mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}. $$

The basic idea behind this theorem is that quantum computation can simulate classical randomized computation. Without going into too much detail, classical computation can be implemented using reversible quantum gates, while randomness can be generated using Hadamard gates: $$ H|0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle). $$ Measuring this qubit produces $0$ or $1$, each with probability $1/2$. This is fundamentally what allows us to simulate random coin flips, and therefore any probabilistic classical computation.

However, we do not know whether the reverse inclusion holds. In fact, it is widely believed that $$ \mathsf{BPP}\subsetneq\mathsf{BQP}. $$ In other words, we think that quantum computation can solve some problems efficiently that classical randomized computation cannot.

We also know that $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$. An equivalent result holds for quantum computation:

Theorem. $$ \mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}. $$
The proof is more involved than the proof that $\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$, but the underlying idea is similar: we can consider exponentially many possibilities one at a time, reusing memory. Because a $n$-qubit quantum state is represented by a vector of length $2^n$, we cannot directly store the entire state. Instead, we can compute individual amplitudes recursively, considering the contributions of different computational paths one at a time. By reusing memory between these computations, we can calculate the final measurement probabilities with high precision using only polynomial space.

Combining these results with classical results gives $$ \boxed{ \mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BPP} \subseteq\mathsf{BQP} \subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE} \subseteq\mathsf{EXP}. } $$

Finally, what is the relationship between $\mathsf{NP}$ and $\mathsf{BQP}$? This is actually still an open question. We do not know whether $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$, nor do we know whether $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$, similar to the case with $\mathsf{BPP}$ and $\mathsf{NP}$.

The Strong Church–Turing Thesis

Recall the Church–Turing Thesis, which states that any function that can be computed by an effective algorithm can also be computed by a Turing machine. In other words, Turing machines capture our intuitive idea of what it means for a function to be computable.

However, the Church–Turing Thesis says nothing about the efficiency of a computation. Even if a Turing machine can simulate another computational model, this simulation might require significantly more time. This motivates a stronger version of the thesis.

The Strong Church–Turing Thesis. Every physically feasible model of computation can be simulated by a probabilistic Turing machine with at most a polynomial increase in running time.

Quantum computing, however, challenges this thesis. As we have seen, quantum computers operate according to different principles when compared to classical computers, using ideas like superposition and entanglement. Although quantum computations can be simulated by classical computers, we do not know whether they can always be simulated with only polynomial overhead.

In fact, if we could prove that $$ \mathsf{BPP}\subsetneq\mathsf{BQP}, $$ which we think is the case, we would show that there exist problems solvable efficiently by quantum computers but not by any probabilistic Turing machine. Assuming quantum computers are physically feasible, this would contradict the Strong Church–Turing Thesis.

While we have stated before that we have been unable to prove such a separatio so far, quantum algorithms do provide some evidence that quantum computation can offer advantages over classical computation. One example is Grover's algorithm, which we will discuss in detail in the next section.
